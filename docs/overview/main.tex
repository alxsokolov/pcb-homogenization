{\LARGE\bfseries pcb\_homogenization}\\[2pt]
{\small\color{muted} PCB copper-layout homogenization and solder-joint thermal-cycling simulation
\textperiodcentered{} module overview with example results}

\subsection*{What it does}
A 2-layer PCB layout (Gerber) is rasterized into copper/FR4 maps, coarse-grained by a quadtree
with per-leaf Voigt--Reuss homogenization, and mapped onto an ANSYS MAPDL board model. A partner-supplied
CR0805 resistor is placed on the board with SAC305 solder, the assembly is driven through a reflow and
thermal-cycling profile with creep, and the solder creep strain is post-processed per cycle as input for a
fatigue-life law.

\subsection*{Pipeline}
\colorbox{codebg}{\parbox{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep}{\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}l@{\quad}l@{\quad}l@{}}
1 & \code{import\_real\_board.py}  & Gerber $\rightarrow$ sample folder \\
2 & \code{discretize.py}              & copper rasters $\rightarrow$ \code{mesh/F\_Cu\_map.npy}, \code{B\_Cu\_map.npy} \\
3 & \code{quadtree.py}                & Voigt--Reuss leaves $\rightarrow$ \code{mesh/quadtree.json} \\
4 & \code{simulate\_pyansys\_assembly.py} & MAPDL solve $\rightarrow$ \code{results/pyansys\_assembly.rst}, \code{thermal\_profile.yaml/.png} \\
5 & \code{zone\_creep\_strain.py}     & critical-zone creep strain $\rightarrow$ \code{results/zone\_creep\_strain.json} \\
6 & \code{plot\_cycle\_creep\_increment.py} & $\Delta\varepsilon$ per cycle $\rightarrow$ \code{results/cycle\_creep\_increment.json} \\
\end{tabular}
}}

\subsection*{Example used throughout}
Board \code{rv3032\_example} (\qtyproduct{20 x 15}{\milli\metre}, Cu \SI{35}{\micro\metre} / FR4 \SI{1.53}{\milli\metre}),
solved with the shortened example profile \code{configs/thermal\_profile\_example.yaml}
(cool-down from the stress-free \SI{180}{\celsius} to \SI{22}{\celsius} with a \SI{300}{\second} hold, then one
\SIrange{-40}{125}{\celsius} cycle), a \numproduct{20 x 20} board mesh and 8 cores: about \SI{8.5}{\minute}
wall time, 60 substeps, \SI{0.6}{\giga\byte} result file.

\section{Board input}
\mod{import\_real\_board.py} converts an externally authored board into the common sample layout
(\code{metadata.json} + \code{gerber/}): it copies the top and bottom copper Gerbers, shifts them so the board
starts at (0,0), and records the stackup. Only the two outer copper layers and the dielectric core are kept.

\section{Discretization}
\mod{discretize.py} parses each RS-274X copper layer (pads, traces, regions) and rasterizes it onto a
uniform grid (\code{-{}-resolution 40} px/mm $\rightarrow$ \numproduct{800 x 600} px here; default 10) and writes \code{F\_Cu\_map.npy},
\code{B\_Cu\_map.npy} and \code{discretization.json}. The top layer carries the signal traces and the
resistor pads (R1, blue markers); the bottom layer is a ground pour with clearances.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/input_layers.png}
  \caption{Input copper layers of \code{rv3032\_example} after rasterization. Blue crosses: R1 pad centres
  used for component placement (\code{solder\_reliability.py}).}
  \label{fig:layers}
\end{figure}

\section{Homogenization (quadtree)}
\mod{quadtree.py} splits the board wherever a leaf straddles a copper/FR4 boundary (pure leaves are already
exact) and enforces 2:1 balance, or alternatively stops at a leaf budget (\code{-{}-max-leaves}). Each leaf
gets Voigt (in-plane) and Reuss (out-of-plane) stiffness and CTE from its top/bottom copper fractions
(\code{homogenization.voigt\_reuss\_bounds()}). Output: \code{mesh/quadtree.json}.

The MAPDL board model does not use these homogenized properties: it projects the layout directly onto its
elements (Section~\ref{sec:mapping}). \code{mesh/quadtree.json} currently feeds only the quick analytical
CTE-mismatch estimate in \mod{solder\_reliability.py}.

\section{Trace mapping onto the MAPDL board mesh}
\label{sec:mapping}
\mod{simulate\_pyansys\_benchmark.py} builds a structured hex mesh of the board (bottom Cu / FR4 core /
top Cu; nodes and elements created directly via PyMAPDL) and paints each element copper or FR4 by sampling
the raster at the element centre. The mesh density (\code{-{}-nx}, \code{-{}-ny}) therefore decides how much of
the layout survives: at \numproduct{50 x 50} (\qtyproduct{0.4 x 0.3}{\milli\metre} elements) the traces are still
recognizable, at \numproduct{20 x 20} most thin traces vanish and only pads and wide features remain.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/board_model.png}
  \caption{Top copper layout (left) and the top copper layer of the board as it was simulated (right),
  read back from the result file by \code{plot\_board\_model.py}: \numproduct{20 x 20} elements, each pure
  copper or pure FR4 (no homogenization). Outlines: the simulated CR0805 body (MAT 2), terminations (MAT 6)
  and solder (MAT 9); red crosses: the R1 pad centres used for placement. For a homogenized board the right panel shows the per-element
  in-plane modulus as a colormap instead.}
  \label{fig:mapping}
\end{figure}

\section{Component and solder}
\mod{component\_import.py} reads the partner-supplied CR0805 archive
(\code{DemonstratorB\_CR0805\_Export.cdb}) into the running MAPDL session without remeshing it. Board
materials are moved to MAT 9001/9002 first so they cannot collide with the archive's own material IDs, and
the SAC305 solder law (elastic + creep) is applied from \code{SAC305\_MikroVal.mac}.
\mod{simulate\_pyansys\_assembly.py} places the component at the midpoint of the two R1 pads
(\code{PAD\_1\_XY\_MM}/\code{PAD\_2\_XY\_MM} in \code{solder\_reliability.py}, taken from the Gerber), turns it
onto the pad axis (here \SI{90}{\degree}), drops the solder onto the board's top surface and connects it via MPC
bonded contact, reusing the TARGE170/CONTA174 template from the archive. R1 is an 0603 footprint
(\qtyproduct{0.55 x 0.40}{\milli\metre} pads, \SI{1.65}{\milli\metre} apart) while the archive is a CR0805, so
the component overhangs the pads; Fig.~\ref{fig:mapping} shows its outline on the layout.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering

\section{Thermal load}
\mod{thermal\_profile.py} reads a list of (time, temperature) points and applies them as a uniform
temperature field from a MAPDL time table, so every substep sees the exact profile temperature; each segment
is solved as its own load step. TREF is \SI{180}{\celsius}, the upper end of the solder's material data, and
the example profile starts there, so the solve begins from an undeformed, stress-free state.
The profile is plotted before the solve (\code{results/thermal\_profile.png}, or standalone via
\code{python thermal\_profile.py <profile.yaml>}) and saved next to the results; its cold-hold starts are the
cycle boundaries used below.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\linewidth]{figures/thermal_profile.png}
  \caption{Applied load of the example profile: cool-down from \SI{180}{\celsius} in \SI{600}{\second},
  \SI{300}{\second} hold at room temperature, then one thermal cycle with \SI{300}{\second} holds. Markers are
  load-step ends; dotted lines are the cycle boundaries.}
  \label{fig:temp}
\end{figure}

\section{Creep post-processing}
All post-processing reads the \code{.rst} directly (ansys-mapdl-reader); no MAPDL session or licence is
needed. \mod{zone\_creep\_strain.py} selects the critical solder sub-volume (here \SI{25}{\percent} of the
solder volume with the highest accumulated creep strain, NL,CREQ) and volume-averages its creep strain over
time, following R.~Schwerz's (Fraunhofer IKTS) APDL macro and Darveaux's method.

  the run (right), both from \code{zone\_creep\_strain.py}.}
  \label{fig:zone}

\mod{plot\_cycle\_creep\_increment.py} cuts the zone-averaged history at the thermal-cycle boundaries and
reports the creep-strain increment per cycle, the $\Delta\varepsilon$ that enters a Coffin--Manson-type
fatigue law. Plotting every cycle shows whether the response has stabilized (shakedown) before a value is
picked.

  \end{subfigure}

  $\Delta\varepsilon = \num{8.4e-3}$ for the single cycle of the example run.}

The example run demonstrates the workflow. With one cycle, short holds and a coarse \numproduct{20 x 20}
board mesh that loses most traces under the joint (Fig.~\ref{fig:mapping}), its $\Delta\varepsilon$ is not a
fatigue value: use the full profile, several cycles and the default mesh for that.

\section{Board model and mesh resolution: a summary}
\label{sec:comparison}
Three questions, answered together: does the quadtree leaf count matter, does smearing the two
copper layers into one through-thickness material vs.\ resolving them separately
(\code{--board-model homogenized} vs.\ \code{homogenized\_zlayers}) matter, and does any of that
survive mesh refinement? \mod{plot\_delta\_summary.py} collects the per-cycle $\Delta\varepsilon$ of
eight \mod{simulate\_pyansys\_assembly.py} runs spanning both board-model families and three mesh
resolutions, read back from their saved \code{results/} JSON (no \code{.rst} access needed) --
one figure instead of the three separate time-series-plus-bar comparisons an earlier version of
this report built up one at a time.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/delta_summary.png}
  \caption{Per-cycle $\Delta\varepsilon$ across the study's board-model and mesh-resolution
  combinations. \textbf{Leaf count barely matters}: at the coarse \numproduct{20 x 20}/
  \code{nz\_per\_layer=2} mesh, 241 vs.\ 19 smeared leaves land within \SI{0.1}{\percent} of each
  other ($\num{9.00e-3}$ both); at the fine \numproduct{20 x 20}/\code{nz\_per\_layer=10} mesh, one
  leaf per element (400 leaves, $z$-resolved, \num{7.44e-3}) sits close to the 19-leaf $z$-resolved
  result at the coarser mesh (\num{8.06e-3}). \textbf{Smeared vs.\ $z$-resolved is a real but
  modest effect}: smeared leaves sit $\sim$\SI{6.5}{\percent} above the matching projection
  (\num{9.00e-3} vs.\ \num{8.45e-3}), $z$-resolved $\sim$\SI{5}{\percent} below it
  (\num{7.44e-3}--\num{8.06e-3} vs.\ \num{7.87e-3}--\num{8.45e-3}) -- opposite signs, similar
  magnitude, regardless of leaf count. \textbf{Mesh resolution dominates both}: refining
  \numproduct{20 x 20}$\to$\numproduct{50 x 50} drops $\Delta\varepsilon$ by \SI{40}{\percent}
  (\num{7.87e-3}$\to$\num{4.72e-3}, projection) -- confirmed to be a genuine mesh effect, not a
  material-resolution one, by re-applying the \numproduct{20 x 20}-leaf material to the
  \numproduct{50 x 50} mesh unchanged (\num{4.77e-3}, within \SI{1.1}{\percent} of the full
  \numproduct{50 x 50} projection). The \numproduct{20 x 20} mesh is coarse enough to lose or
  badly distort the thin traces right under the solder joint (visible already in
  Fig.~\ref{fig:mapping}), so neither coarse-mesh result -- projection or homogenized -- was close
  to converged to begin with.}
  \label{fig:comparison}
\end{figure}

Whether \numproduct{50 x 50} itself has converged is still open -- the next check would be
\numproduct{80 x 80} or finer, at the same \code{nz\_per\_layer=10}.

\section{Leaf resolution vs.\ mesh resolution, swept together}
\label{sec:leaf-sweep}
\mod{plot\_leaf\_resolution\_sweep.py} puts two different sweeps on one plot. The \emph{homogenized}
series re-applies \code{quadtree.py --per-element-grid N N} for $N \in \{5, 10, 25, 50\}$ to the
\emph{same} \numproduct{50 x 50}, \code{nz\_per\_layer=10} FE mesh throughout -- an in-plane
\emph{material}-resolution sweep, isolating the homogenization leaf count with the mesh held fixed
(\S\ref{sec:comparison}'s question). $N$ must divide \numproduct{50 x 50} evenly for every
leaf to land on a whole number of elements ($N=20$ gives \num{2.5} elements per leaf, so leaf
boundaries fall mid-element and were dropped from this version of the plot for that reason); $N \in
\{5, 10, 25, 50\}$ are 50's own divisors. The \emph{projection} series instead re-meshes the binary
copper/FR4 board itself at $N \in \{20, 30, 50\}$ in-plane, same \code{nz\_per\_layer=10} -- an
in-plane \emph{mesh}-resolution sweep, the question \S\ref{sec:comparison}'s single
\numproduct{20 x 20}-vs-\numproduct{50 x 50} comparison first raised. The two series share only the
plot, not an $x$-axis meaning: $N$ is a leaf count for one, a mesh element count for the other.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{figures/leaf_resolution_sweep.png}
  \caption{Per-cycle $\Delta\varepsilon$ vs.\ resolution $N$: the homogenized leaf sweep (orange,
  mesh fixed at \numproduct{50 x 50}) against the binary projection's own mesh sweep (green). The
  projection series swings by \SI{67}{\percent} peak-to-peak as its mesh refines ($\num{7.87e-3}$
  at $N=20$, $\num{4.98e-3}$ at $N=30$, $\num{4.72e-3}$ at $N=50$) -- confirming
  \S\ref{sec:comparison}'s finding with a third point: mesh convergence, not a two-point
  artifact. The homogenized series barely moves across the same range ($\num{4.687e-3}$ to
  $\num{4.770e-3}$, under \SI{2}{\percent} end to end) and does \emph{not} converge back onto the
  projection series -- the two land within \SI{1}{\percent} of each other only because both happen
  to approach the same mesh-converged value by $N=50$, not because either curve is heading toward
  the other. The conclusion is the same as \S\ref{sec:comparison}'s: FE mesh resolution is the
  dominant error source in this model by a wide margin; the binary-vs-homogenized modeling choice
  is a comparatively small, and not mesh-resolution-dependent, second-order effect.}
  \label{fig:leaf-sweep}
\end{figure}

\section{Sensitivity to the copper layer's apparent z-thickness}
The real copper layers are \SI{35}{\micro\metre} thick -- far thinner than one FR4-core element at
this mesh (\code{nz\_per\_layer=10} over the \SI{1.53}{\milli\metre} core gives \SI{153}{\micro\metre}
elements, already $\sim$\num{4.4}\texttimes\ the true copper thickness). A new
\code{--cu-penetration-elements N} option on \mod{simulate\_pyansys\_assembly.py} asks how much that
mismatch matters: it artificially extends, $N$ extra FR4-core elements deep from each surface, the
region treated as copper-bearing, with the mesh, in-plane raster/leaf grid and everything else
unchanged. $N=0$ matches the physical stackup exactly and reuses \S\ref{sec:leaf-sweep}'s $N=50$
points (\code{rv3032\_projection\_fine}, \code{rv3032\_homog\_leaf50}); $N \in \{1, 3, 5\}$ were run
fresh for both board models, six solves at the \numproduct{50 x 50}/\code{nz\_per\_layer=10} mesh.

The two board models cannot treat that extra depth the same way. Binary projection paints each
element wholly copper or wholly FR4 -- it has no way to represent a thinner copper film within a
thicker element, so painting the penetrated rows copper (the raster's own pattern, repeated at each
depth) inevitably adds copper volume that is not physically there, growing with $N$.
\code{homogenized\_zlayers} \emph{can} represent a fractional copper content, so
\code{orthotropic\_engineering\_constants\_layer()}'s new \code{dilution} argument keeps that volume
fixed instead: the leaf's true phi is scaled by $t_\mathrm{Cu}/(t_\mathrm{Cu} + N\,t_\mathrm{core})$
before the Voigt--Reuss mix, smearing the same real copper amount over the thicker effective region
rather than repeating it at full density. (An earlier version of this sweep skipped that step and
just reused each leaf's true-thickness material across the penetrated rows too -- which reproduced
binary's volume inflation instead of avoiding it, and was corrected before the results below.)

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{figures/cu_penetration_sweep.png}
  \caption{Per-cycle $\Delta\varepsilon$ vs.\ copper penetration depth $N$ (FR4-core elements),
  binary projection (green) vs.\ homogenized $z$-resolved with volume-conserving dilution (orange),
  \numproduct{50 x 50}/\code{nz\_per\_layer=10} mesh fixed throughout. The two models could hardly
  diverge more sharply: binary rises steeply and non-linearly with $N$ ($+36\,\%$ at $N=1$,
  $+166\,\%$ at $N=3$, $+339\,\%$ at $N=5$), while homogenized stays essentially flat
  ($-5.7\,\%$, $-8.4\,\%$, $-7.0\,\%$ -- a small, non-monotonic wobble, not a trend). This is the
  expected consequence of the dilution factor itself collapsing fast ($t_\mathrm{Cu}/(t_\mathrm{Cu}
  + N\,t_\mathrm{core}) = 0.19, 0.07, 0.04$ at $N=1,3,5$): the penetrated region's effective
  material converges toward plain FR4 almost immediately, so adding more of it changes little.
  Binary projection's steep climb is therefore less a statement about the board's real physics than
  about a structural limitation of binary (non-fractional) material assignment once the FE mesh is
  coarser than the feature it represents -- a limitation \code{homogenized\_zlayers} does not share,
  provided the homogenization itself conserves volume as the effective layer thickness changes.}
  \label{fig:cu-penetration}
\end{figure}
